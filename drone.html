1 · Identify the plant and target

This experiment uses a normalised teaching plant with damping and a restoring term. The target is a 10 m step.

\[ G_1(s)=\frac{K_t}{Js^2+Bs+K} =\frac{1}{s^2+2s+1} \]

Here, \(J=1,\ B=2,\ K=1,\ K_t=1\). Without disturbance, \(\ddot y=u-2\dot y-y\).

Keep the same plant, target and Kₚ for the four tests. Use positive Kᵢ for PI and PID, and positive Kᴅ for PD and PID, so each controller includes its intended actions.

5 · Explain the differences: P vs PD vs PI vs PID

Compare all four wind-free tests at the same Kₚ. Separate final tracking error from transient behaviour.

Controller Expected steady-state behaviour What to compare
P A stable response settles below 10 m. For this plant, \(e_{ss}=10/(1+K_P)\). Use P as the baseline. A nonzero error produces the constant input balancing the plant’s restoring term.
PD At the same Kₚ, PD has the same theoretical final altitude and step-reference error as P. Derivative action changes the transient response. Compare oscillation, overshoot and damping with P. It does not remove this plant’s constant step offset.
PI With Kᵢ > 0 and a stable closed loop, altitude approaches 10 m and error approaches zero. Integral action removes the constant step offset. Compare its transient behaviour with P and PD. PI is not automatically faster or less oscillatory.
PID With Kᵢ > 0 and a stable closed loop, altitude also approaches 10 m with zero final error. Compare PID with PI to investigate derivative action while retaining integral action. The gains determine whether overshoot and settling improve.

After recording all four: Do P and PD have similar final errors at the same Kₚ? Do stable PI and PID responses remove that error? Use the graphs to explain their transient differences.

With Kᵢ = 0, PI behaves as P and PID behaves as PD. With Kᴅ = 0, PD behaves as P and PID behaves as PI.

6 · Optional: test disturbance rejection

Complete the four wind-free recordings first. Then apply the constant downward disturbance after settling.

P and PD generally retain an additional altitude offset. PI and PID can reject a constant disturbance when integral action is active and the closed loop is stable.

Reference models and feedback diagram

Reference models and response formulas

First-order reference model

\[\frac{Y(s)}{R(s)}=\frac{1}{\tau s+1}\]

Standard second-order reference model

\[ \frac{Y(s)}{R(s)} =\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2} \]

Third-order reference model

\[ \frac{Y(s)}{R(s)} =\frac{A}{(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2)(s+p)} \]

Standard underdamped second-order response

\[ \omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}, \qquad\theta=\cos^{-1}(\zeta) \] \[ t_r=\frac{\pi-\theta}{\omega_d}, \qquad t_p=\frac{\pi}{\omega_d} \] \[ M_p=100e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\%, \qquad t_s\approx\frac{4}{\zeta\omega_n} \]

These formulas apply to the standard stable underdamped second-order model. Controller zeros and higher-order dynamics can change the complete response.

5. Mathematics behind the flight

Altitude tracking error
\(e(t)=r(t)-y(t)\)
Controller command
\(u(t)=K_pe(t)+K_i\int_0^t e(\tau)\,d\tau+K_d\dfrac{de(t)}{dt}\)
Vertical plant
\(\ddot y=u-2\dot y-y+d(t)\)
Live numerical simulation
\(v_{k+1}=v_k+a_k\Delta t\)
\(y_{k+1}=y_k+v_{k+1}\Delta t\)

\(r\) is the 10 m target, \(y\) is altitude and \(d(t)\) is the disturbance. The analysis graph uses the complete wind-free closed-loop transfer function. The live drone uses the time-domain plant equations. Live derivative action uses altitude velocity to avoid a reference-step derivative kick.
