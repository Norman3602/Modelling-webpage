4 · Understand your results

Why can the design values and live values differ?

The design equations use a dominant second-order approximation. The live graph measures the complete PID closed-loop response, which also contains the third pole and PID zeros. Because of this, the measured rise time, peak time, overshoot and settling time do not have to exactly match the design prediction.

5 · What to expect: PD vs PI vs PID

Compare the wind-free step responses first. Use the same 10 m target, plant and proportional gain for all three runs. Watch the altitude curve, current error and recorded steady-state error. The exact transient shape depends on your gains.

Controller What you should observe What it means
P Settles below 10 m with a nonzero step-reference error. Proportional action alone does not eliminate the step error for this comparison plant.
PD The altitude may settle below 10 m. The current error approaches a nonzero value. Derivative action affects the transient response but does not, by itself, eliminate the constant step-reference error for this comparison plant. Compare the observed error with the theoretical value.
PI With stable gains, the altitude approaches 10 m and the current error approaches zero. The rise and settling behaviour may differ from PD. Integral action accumulates the error and can remove the constant step-reference offset. Look for any oscillation or overshoot rather than assuming PI is always faster.
PID With stable gains, the altitude also approaches 10 m and the current error approaches zero. Its overshoot and settling time may differ from PI. Integral action removes the step offset; derivative action changes the transient response. Compare the actual curves and measured values rather than assuming PID always gives the smallest overshoot.

After recording all three: Check whether each observed steady-state error agrees with its theoretical value. Then compare rise time, overshoot and settling time from the graphs. If a response has not settled or is unstable, do not record it as a valid steady-state result.

Reference models and feedback diagram

Reference models and response formulas

First-order reference model

\[\frac{Y(s)}{R(s)}=\frac{1}{\tau s+1}\]

Standard second-order reference model

\[\frac{Y(s)}{R(s)}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}\]

Third-order reference model

\[\frac{Y(s)}{R(s)}=\frac{A}{(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2)(s+p)}\]

Standard underdamped second-order response

\[\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2},\quad\theta=\cos^{-1}(\zeta)\] \[t_r=\frac{\pi-\theta}{\omega_d},\quad t_p=\frac{\pi}{\omega_d}\] \[M_p=100e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\%,\quad t_s\approx\frac{4}{\zeta\omega_n}\]

These response-index formulas apply to the standard stable underdamped second-order model. PD controller zeros and third-order PI/PID dynamics can change the actual response; use the simulator measurements for those cases.

5. Mathematics behind the flight

Altitude tracking error
\(e(t)=r(t)-y(t)\)
Controller command
\(u(t)=K_pe(t)+K_i\int_0^t e(\tau)\,d\tau+K_d\dfrac{de(t)}{dt}\)
Simplified vertical plant
\(\ddot y(t)=\omega_0^2u(t)-2\zeta_0\omega_0\dot y(t)+d(t)\)
Numerical simulation
\(v_{k+1}=v_k+a_k\Delta t\)
\(y_{k+1}=y_k+v_{k+1}\Delta t\)

Here, \(r\) is the target altitude (10 m), \(y\) is actual altitude, and \(d(t)\) is the applied vertical disturbance. The takeoff-analysis graph is calculated without wind. For Experiment 2 PID design, it uses the complete third-order closed-loop transfer function from the lecture-note derivation. The live drone remains a time-domain visualisation of the selected plant and controller.
